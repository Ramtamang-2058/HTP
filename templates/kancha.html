Robotics · InMoov Humanoid

Kancha.

A voice-controlled humanoid robot built at the High Tech Pioneer Lab in Kathmandu. You talk to it the way you'd talk to a person — it hears you, sees the room around it, and answers with conversation, a gesture, or a movement.

The robot that reasons in the moment

Not scripted. Decided live.

Kancha listens for a wake word, converts your speech to text, and uses a large language model to decide how to respond — as conversation, a vision query, or one of 30+ physical gestures. Most InMoov builds worldwide run pre-scripted routines; Kancha's behaviour is chosen in the moment, not looked up from a script.

Built on the open-source InMoov platform, it addresses a gap in Nepal's robotics landscape by integrating real-time voice, LLM reasoning, computer vision and physical response into a single working pipeline.

How it works

One continuous pipeline: hear → think → see → move.

  1. 01

    Wake word & speech

    An offline Vosk wake word opens a conversation window, then Whisper turns your speech into text — English today, Nepali in the works.

  2. 02

    LLM intent reasoning

    GPT-4o-mini routes every utterance to one of three intents — conversation, a vision query, or a physical gesture — and picks the response.

  3. 03

    Computer vision

    A Jetson Nano runs face recognition (InsightFace), live object detection (YOLOv8) and on-demand scene description (GPT-4o) — so Kancha knows who is in the room and what's on the table.

  4. 04

    Physical response

    26 servos across arms, hands, head and jaw perform the gesture; a wheeled base driven by an ESP32-S3 moves and turns, auto-stopping on obstacles.

Key features

What Kancha can actually do.

Natural voice interaction

Wake-word detection and Whisper speech-to-text; a 30-second window means you don't repeat the wake word mid-conversation. Say “Stop” anytime.

LLM-driven intent

One model decides the response: a conversation, a vision lookup, or any of 30+ gestures — wave, handshake, fist bump, namaste, salute and more.

Continuous vision

Face recognition, live YOLO object detection and on-demand GPT-4o scene description, all injected into every conversation as room awareness.

26-servo movement

Arms, hands, fingers, head, eyes and jaw articulate naturally — including a jaw that moves for speech, and gesture libraries people actually use.

Mobile base with obstacle avoidance

A single ESP32-S3 drives the motors and auto-stops on any of four ultrasonics; encoder- and gyroscope-based distance and heading tracking is in final calibration for full closed-loop navigation.

Built for Nepal

Designed for students, museum and event visitors, and researchers exploring embodied AI — enough that a Nepali person can interact with it physically.

Why it stands out

A Nepali successor to Nepal's first robot.

Kancha is built at HTP Lab, the same lab founded by Muni Bahadur Sakya — Nepal's first computer scientist, who built a Nepali-speaking, obstacle-aware robot in 2004–05 and received the ICT Pioneer Award in 2016.

Kancha continues that lineage — from rule-based robotics to LLM-driven reasoning — a small team working with open hardware to build something otherwise mostly demonstrated by well-funded laboratories abroad.

Anatomy of the build

Every part earned its place by hand.

What looks like a friendly face is a stack of quietly hard-won engineering — a wheeled base that measures its own distance, legs milled to fit, hands with independent fingers, and a head whose camera doubles as its eyes. A portrait of the build, part by part.

Three-year vision

Where Kancha is headed.

  1. Y1

    Out into the world

    Deploy in real demonstration settings — STEM education, exhibitions and public events — while improving durability, including encoder- and stepper-based servos.

  2. Y2

    Speaks Nepali natively

    Extend native Nepali speech input and output, so Kancha holds a full conversation in Nepali — building on Whisper's multilingual support.

  3. Y3

    Intelligence, decoupled

    Separate the AI decision layer from the humanoid body so the same reasoning adapts to other forms — starting with a lower-cost desktop version for schools.
